subsubsection{Stray light findings}
\label{ssec:stray}

The compact, 3-mirror design of the Simonyi Survey Telescope is especially sensitive to stray and scattered light.
Stray light is commonly classified as light that does not follow the nominal optical path within the telescope and instrument.
Stray light can originate from scattered light by optical and non-optical surfaces, and from ghost light paths within refractive components like lenses and filters.
The most pronounced and extended stray light artifacts were observed in the \gls{LSSTCam} images when the boresight angle of the telescope was in proximity of bright objects such as the Moon, bright stars, planets or unintended light sources on the sky.
Stray light contaminates all astronomical imaging to some level, but the unprecedented low-surface brightness science that is targeted by \gls{LSST} is especially sensitive to these features.
A significant fraction of these artifacts was back-traced to the Observatory dome status, which is missing its \gls{LWS} to restrict the acceptance angle along the elevation axis of the telescope.
Upon completion and commissioning of the \gls{LWS}, many stray light artifacts are expected to resolve spontaneously without further mitigation actions, while others will require some extra baffling solutions.
Part of the stray light features detected were expected from an initial stray light analysis study in FRED\registered \cite{2009SPIE.7427E..08E}, which included the full opto-mechanical model of the telescope and dome.
Such a study led to the design and development of several light baffles around the Rubin telescope mirrors and camera.
The study correctly foresaw the existence of the so-called direct paths from the primary (M1) and tertiary (M3) mirror of the telescope: these optical paths consist of lights reflected off the M1/M3 surfaces, bypassing the secondary mirror, and hitting directly the LSSTCam optics and focal plane.
Other artifacts were new and unexpected, and they required intensive visual inspection of the LSSTCam images, dedicated ray tracing simulation and on-sky tests to infer and confirm their origins \cite{2026arXiv2606.31945}.
The investigations of the stray light origin were mainly conducted using the Ansys Zemax OpticStudio\registered software, which allows importation and modeling mechanical components of the telescope, camera, and dome.
Already from the engineering first photon with the Rubin Commissioning Camera (\gls{ComCam}), some level of stray light was detected associated in correspondence with bright astronomical objects.
The \gls{ComCam} pathfinder optical tests helped developing a team and several testing scripts for addressing stray light issues.
During the Rubin on-sky commissioning period with \gls{LSSTCam} (\svcampaignstartdate -- \dptwoenddate), the stray light test campaign was organized in two subsets: tests conducted on-sky, and in-dome testing using the \gls{calibration} light sources.
About $\sim$38 test cases were issued and executed for conducting stray light studies with Rubin, cumulatively accounting for more than 14 hours on-sky and 19 hours in-dome.
The majority of the stray light tests involved a sequence of telescope pointing and \gls{camera} lateral and/or rotational offsets to place and raster a bright object in specific off-axis positions where stray light features were observed or suspected to appear. A large fraction of the observed stray light features were discovered serendipitously while performing other engineering tests or scientific observations.
An extensive visual inspection campaign was carried out by the stray light team since the first astronomical photons.
The screening consisted of regular visual inspections of the focal plane mosaic images corrected for instrument signatures but not \gls{background}-subtracted.
On-sky and calibration images were inspected on a daily to weekly cadence, and the stray light artifacts were identified in the images and logged. Subsequently, the image metadata was augmented with information about the location of the dome, locations of the Moon and bright stars. These data served the important purpose of understanding occurrence rates and plausible light sources and of retrieving the angular off-axis coordinates of the bright objects to reproduce the stray light scenario in the ray tracing simulations.
In-dome studies of stray light were carried out regularly during bad weather nights using the \gls{CBP} (Rubin calibration tool \cite{Mondrik_2023}) to inject collimated light beams within a small fraction of the telescope pupil at different wavelengths and off-axis angles. In this context, the CBP was also used to model the LSSTCam optical ghosts as reported in \cite{2026arXiv2606.31826}.

An initial census of the observed stray light artifacts observed in the LSSTCam image frames during the initial commissioning and observations (April 2025 - May 2026) is reproduced in \tabref{tab:rates} from \cite{2026arXiv2606.31945}.
The $\sim$70\% of the features come from already identified and confirmed sources, and $\sim$80\% of the artifacts have already a possible mitigation strategy.
The occurrence rate is based on a visual inspection and classification of the features visible by eye in the image frames; for some features, the fractional area is a rough estimate because their extent varies across different image frames.
The average fractional area can be compared to the maximum percentage of pixel loss at the LSSTCam focal plane ($<$4\% by the end of the 10-year survey) and the minimum area covered by science-grade imaging devices ($>$ 85\% over a 3.5-degree FoV) \cite{LSE-30}.

The most frequent and impactful stray light feature observed in the LSSTCam image frames during on-sky commissioning is the so-called \textit{scratched tape} feature \cite{2026arXiv2606.31939}.
This artifact originates from an M1 direct path that skips reflections on M2 and M3 as foreseen from the initial study by \cite{2009SPIE.7427E..08E}.
The \gls{LWS} will naturally shield this stray light path from the sky, but due to the delay in its deployment and completion, this feature was visually apparent in approximately 7.4\% of the image frames, with an off-axis range of visibility between $\sim$19\fdeg8 and 22$^\circ$.
Once the origin of the feature was understood through on-sky, and in-dome testing, an annular extension of the telescope mid-level baffle ring was developed to block this stray light.
The baffle extension was installed between February and April 2026, and the LSSTCam images no longer suffer from this prominent stray light feature as reported in \cite{2026arXiv2606.31939}.
There are other stray light features, like the so-called \textit{L3 Horseshoe}, coming from edge diffraction phenomena at the tertiary lens (L3) of LSSTCam in the outskirt of its field of view ($\sim1\fdeg99$ and $2\fdeg23$).
The Rubin stray light team is studying the feasibility of installing an additional light baffle in front of the L3, and a detailed opto-mechanical analysis of the design development is reported by \cite{2026arXiv2606.31898}.
Intensive ray tracing simulations led also to discover scattered light from the M2 optical baffle which is made of black-anodized Aluminum, and it has a residual reflectance in the longer wavelength bands ($i$, $z$, $y$) that can reach up to 30\%.
The scattered background light is visually evident only when triggered by the Moon, for off-axis angles between $12^\circ$ and $20^\circ$.
A detailed analysis of the light scattered by the M2 baffle is reported by \cite{2026arXiv2606.31793}.
This stray light optical path will not be blocked by the \gls{LWS}; however, the reflectance of the M2 baffle can be decreased if the surface is coated with Aeroglaze\registered Z306.
In summary, the initial 2006 stray light study  \cite{2009SPIE.7427E..08E} resulted in approximately 50 new design requirements to prevent stray light, including wedged optical windows, anti-reflection coatings, blackened adjacent surfaces, and a dome interior engineered for very low reflectivity.
Despite these rigorous preventative measures, about 10\% of early image frames suffered from stray light contamination.  The most severe issues were structured artifacts on the focal plane, largely stemming from the incomplete status of the LWS.
A tiger team of experts facilitated  the construction and installation of an additional annular baffle around the telescope which fully resolved the most prominent and frequent stray light features in the LSSTCam images \citep{2026arXiv2606.31939}.
To address part of the other unexpected artifacts, the Rubin team is actively implementing new physical baffling and re-painting \citep{2026arXiv2606.31898, 2026arXiv2606.31793}.
