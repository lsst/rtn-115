\section{Performance Characterization and Known Issues}
\label{sec:performance}

In this section, we present a characterization of the performance of the \gls{DP2} data products and highlight known issues.
The analyses presented here provide an overview of the data quality rather than a comprehensive validation.
Further information on the performance and known issues of \gls{DP2} can be found in the Data Quality, Image Artifacts, and Known Issues sections of the Release Documentation at \url{https://dp2.lsst.io}, which will continue to be updated as our understanding of the data and its nuances improves.

\subsection{Sensor Anomalies}

Some features in \gls{LSSTCam} images are not yet fully corrected by the instrument signature removal (ISR) pipeline.
We describe these artifacts and how we mitigate them.
Where no mitigation exists yet, we document the artifact here so that users can recognize it in the data.

The standard ``hot'' and ``cold'' pixels seen in other detectors are found on \gls{LSSTCam}.
These are single-pixel or few-pixel groups where either the dark current is elevated (resulting in hot pixels) or the quantum efficiency is lower (resulting in cold pixels).
These pixels are identified by searching the combined dark \gls{calibration} for bright pixels to find the hot pixels, and searching the combined flat \gls{calibration} for dark pixels to find the cold pixels.
In addition, we flag any column that has more than 1000 defect pixels within the amplifier segment as entirely bad.
These bad columns usually appear as a hot pixel with a trail extending in the vertical direction, as that bad pixel impacts that column as the amplifier is read out.
The edges of the detectors often have a large number of cold pixels, likely due to edge effects in the electric fields.
Because automatic detection flags these edge pixels inconsistently, we mask a fixed border 7 pixels wide on the top and bottom edges of each detector, and 8 pixels wide on the left and right edges.

As discovered as part of commissioning with \gls{LSSTComCam} for \gls{DP1}, there are also features we've classified as ``vampire'' pixels.
These appear as a very bright hot pixel surrounded by a mostly circular region of low \gls{flux}, such that it appears that the hot pixel is draining the charge from this region.
Although the mechanism of these pixels is not fully understood, the regions appear to conserve charge, so the simple model of the charge being pulled into the central pixel seems reasonable.
We do not currently have a correction for these features, and so have manually masked them with square defect masks.
The current set of manual defects is likely an underestimate, so there may be unmasked vampire pixels in \gls{DP2}.
A more complete scan will be done prior to the next \gls{Data Release}.

Very bright sources can create ``edge bleeds'', where a saturated column exhibits odd behavior as it approaches the edge of the detector where the readout is done.
On the \gls{ITL} detectors, this takes the form of a ``trumpet'' where the saturated column spreads out along the x-direction as it approaches the edge, creating a much wider saturated region.
We have code to detect and mask these trumpet shaped edge bleeds, but from analysis of the \gls{DP2} data, it is clear that this code needs further improvement to ensure that all such bleeds get masked.
The \gls{E2V} detectors show a different kind of edge bleed, in which the saturated columns impact the readout for up to the first 350 rows of the image.
In these rows, pixels read out before the saturated column (in serial readout order) are depressed relative to the rest of the detector, whereas pixels read out after it show \gls{CTI}-like charge smearing.

The \gls{ITL} detectors also show another feature related to saturation: a saturated source that does not extend all the way to the edge can create ``dark dips'' along the saturation trail, where the columns have a lower flux compared to the rest of the detector.
We have measured scale parameters relating the size of the saturated region to the width of the dip, and use that to construct an appropriately sized mask.
Lab studies have suggested that these dips are related to the voltage the detectors receive, and so there may be hardware parameters that can be tuned to reduce or eliminate these features.

In addition to these features that we mask, there are some others that do not yet have a mask or correction.
The \gls{E2V} midline can glow on some images, but we do not yet mask this.
There are low-level ``tree rings'' that are related to how the silicon was sliced during the detector manufacturing process.
These have the effect of slightly changing the effective pixel area, resulting in small scale photometric and astrometric distortions.
We would like to fully characterize these tree rings so we can properly correct for them as part of the flat-fielding step.
Although not formally a ``sensor anomaly,'' there is evidence that the ``comb pattern'' of bright glints at the edge of the detectors is a result of light entering the detector from the side due to small height differences in the installed detectors.
This feature is observed most often in $y$, or in images with higher \gls{background} levels.
This may not be easily corrected, and so an additional detection and edge masking process needs to be created to remove affected areas.

Finally, we identified an unexpected fringing signal in the $z$ and $y$ filters, where the flat correction imprinted a fringe pattern onto the science image.
This indicates that the \gls{calibration} system creates images that have a fringe signal, but that the on-sky observations do not.
Generating a fringe correction to remove this feature will be done prior to the next \gls{Data Release}.

\subsection{Astrometry}
Astrometric performance is characterized by measuring internal consistency as well as accuracy with respect to an external reference, in this case the \gls{Gaia} DR3 catalog.
Per-band metrics are calculated using isolated stars with magnitudes  between 17 and 21.5 in that band.\footnote{While object position does not change with band, object selection is band-specific.}
For this set of objects, we find that the median offset from \gls{Gaia} is within $2$~mas in about $85\%$ of tracts and within $5$~mas for about $99\%$ of tracts.
Note that this calculation is not an independent estimate, since the \gls{Gaia} stars are also used to compute the WCS.
Given that the number of stars used to verify the astrometric solution far exceeds the number that were used to derive it, we do not expect this to have a major effect on our results; an independent calculation is planned for future analyses.
Any offset within the aforementioned numerical margins is believed to be caused by color-dependent selection effects in our \gls{metric} calculation, and is expected to be lower once differential chromatic refraction is included in the astrometric model.

Internal consistency was improved significantly relative to \gls{DP1} by including the Gaussian processes turbulence correction.
The degree of internal consistency can be seen by looking at the ``AMx'' metrics, which show the median over a field of the standard deviation in the separation across all visits between objects at a given distance.
Figure~\ref{fig: amx} shows this \gls{metric} for objects at $5$~arcmin separations and at $200$~arcmin separations.
The top panel shows that \gls{DP2} is reaching the design goal of repeatability below $10$~mas in many regions of the sky.
Furthermore, we are achieving almost the same repeatability at $200$~arcmin separations, for which $10$~mas is actually the stretch goal.

\subsection{Isolated Star Stellar Motions}
Due to the short time span of the DP2 observations, the precision of the proper motion and parallax measurements is highly driven by the external reference catalog, \gls{Gaia} DR3, which is included in the stellar motions calculation where possible.
The majority of isolated stars with magnitudes below about $21$ are in the \gls{Gaia} catalog: for these objects, the proper motion uncertainty is on the scale of a few mas.
For fainter objects for which \gls{Gaia} is not available, on the other hand, even well-sampled objects have proper motion uncertainties on the order of $10-20$~mas, going higher as signal to noise, sampling frequency, and the time span sampled go down.
Regardless, for the majority of tracts, the time spanned between the first and last visits included in \gls{DP2} is less than $100$~days, meaning that proper motion and parallax measurements should be treated as a guideline for what will be available in future Data Releases, rather than a starting point for serious analyses.

\subsection{Differential Chromatic Refraction}
\gls{DCR} is the wavelength-dependent astrometric displacement arising from atmospheric refraction.
The displacement is directed along the parallactic angle and increases toward shorter wavelengths, producing a color-dependent angular offset the size of which differs between bands.

The presence of DCR in LSSTComCam data was previously discussed in the \gls{DP1} release paper \citep[][]{2026AJ....171..360V}.
Here, we characterize the effect in \gls{LSSTCam} data using two representative sky regions: the Extended Chandra Deep Field-South (ECDFS) centered at (R.A., decl.) = (53.16, -28.10) and a field near the ecliptic (Rubin\_SV\_320\_-15) centered at (R.A., decl.) = (320.0, -15.0), respectively.
For each region, we consider all Deep Coadd images from tracts within 5 degrees of the field center.

The extent of any displacement due to DCR is dependent on airmass, meaning that for a \gls{patch} it changes at the per-visit level.
For each visit, we calculate the angular displacement of sources relative to their positions in the matched reference catalog and projected onto the directions parallel and perpendicular to the parallactic angle.
We examine both components as a function of source $g-i$ color; the perpendicular component serves as a control for effects not aligned with the expected \gls{DCR} direction.

When displaying the resulting DCR effect, the coadd with the highest DCR \gls{metric} is selected for each field.
In the case of the \gls{ECDFS}, that specific coadd contains 7 visit images, spanning an airmass range of 1.02--1.36 with a mean of 1.14.
The corresponding ecliptic coadd contains 8 visit images spanning 1.05--1.70 with a mean \gls{airmass} of 1.26.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dcr}, both fields exhibit a color-dependent displacement parallel to the parallactic angle, with no corresponding trend in the perpendicular component.
The color dependence rules out a simple camera-dependent offset and is consistent with the \gls{DCR} signature previously identified in LSSTComCam.
This effect is being addressed through the development of \gls{DCR}-specific templates and coadds designed to mitigate the observed offset in future data products.

\subsection{PSF accuracy}

A full analysis of the PSF characterization of the Rubin Observatory data is currently being written \citep[][]{RTN-126}, so here
we present only the key findings.
One of the key findings is that the PSF shape correlates with variations in height across the focal plane (see \citealt[][]{RTN-108} for more details).
This explains why ITL sensors, which exhibit a curved \gls{shape}, are more difficult to model than E2V's flat sensors, and why a fourth-order model is justified, as noted previously in the \gls{DP1} paper.
As described in \citet[][]{SITCOMTN-174}, the LSSTCam PSF is chromatic (i.e., wavelength-dependent) and appears to be compatible with \gls{DCR}.
As shown in \citet[][]{SITCOMTN-174}, we are able to model it in the same spirit as the DES Y6 PSF analysis, but this was not enabled during DP2 processing because the connections to the downstream processes were not in place.
In addition, DP2 has an order of magnitude more visits than previous processing campaigns, revealing a new variety of \gls{PSF}-related issues, including annealing patterns in E2V sensors — visible in the u- and $g$-band stacks — and fringing in the z- and y-bands.
All are expected to arise from the existence of residual structure after \gls{background} subtraction.
Moreover, \gls{PSF} residuals in DP2 show structure that correlates with stellar density, with the largest biases appearing in the plane of the Milky Way, the LMC, and the SMC.
This stellar-density dependence also explains the observed differences in \gls{PSF} residuals between bands, since more crowded bands show larger residuals.
The underlying cause is again believed to be related to \gls{background} subtraction in dense fields.

\subsection{Deblending}
As mentioned in \secref{sec:drp-multivisit-detection}, a new feature of \gls{DP2} processing is the introduction of a partial deconvolution step prior to deblending.
Including this step significantly decreases the number of identified blends.
As a result, in extragalactic fields such as \gls{COSMOS}, just over half of the objects are not blended in the partially deconvolved image, allowing the deblender to quickly and efficiently recover the flux for objects isolated in observed image and partially deconvolved image.
Most of the remaining blends are small, with only a handful of objects, which \texttt{\gls{scarlet lite}} deblends fairly easily (see \figref{fig:sample_blend}).
Approximately 10\% of objects come from blends with more than 5 children.

The biggest failure modes are resolved grand-design spirals, which are shredded into multiple poorly modeled objects, and undetected objects in the wings of brighter objects.
The deblender's primary limitation is its memory usage, which in crowded fields can become large, and for \gls{DP2} forced us to skip deblending near the Galactic plane and a few other dense regions.
We are currently working on algorithms to allow us to process crowded fields and expect most fields to be successfully processed in future releases.
For users wanting a clean sample of objects that are not blended in the partially deconvolved image, meaning objects that are either fully isolated or that overlap neighbors only in the observed image, we recommend selecting objects with \texttt{deblend\_blendNChild} equal to zero or with \texttt{detect\_isIsolated} set.

\subsection{Crowded Fields}
Crowded fields represent a key challenge for the LSST \gls{Science Pipelines}, pushing the current limits of source detection, deblending, and photometry.
They are defined as regions in which the stellar source density exceeds the resolving capability of current algorithms.
In such regions, the \gls{pipeline} produces large, contiguous detection footprints encompassing multiple astrophysical sources rather than assigning distinct footprints to individual objects; the corresponding pixels are flagged as \texttt{NOT\_DEBLENDED}.
We therefore use the proportion of pixels flagged as \texttt{NOT\_DEBLENDED} as a proxy for the area of the survey that is associated with crowded fields.

Overall, out of the 2.2 trillion pixels comprising the total \gls{DP2} sky area, 12.9\% are associated with crowded fields via the aforementioned definition.
This fraction, however, obscures the highly localized nature of crowded fields within the survey.
Specifically, 1,750 of 2,174 total tracts (i.e., 80.5\%) have less than 1\% of their pixels flagged as \texttt{NOT\_DEBLENDED}.
Of the remaining 424 (i.e., 19.5\%) tracts, 90\% are found within 14.5 degrees of the Galactic plane where the stellar densities are the highest.
The concentration of processing failures in these regions is further illustrated by the fraction of reference sources successfully matched to LSST detections within each tract and \gls{patch}.
\figref{fig:crowded_fields_matched_sources} shows this match ratio across the survey \gls{footprint}.
The lowest match ratios, including tracts and patches with no successful matches, occur almost entirely along the Galactic plane, indicating that these unmatched sources are associated with failures of the standard \gls{LSST} processing in these densely populated fields.
Away from the Galactic plane, match ratios remain uniformly high.
Thus, although crowded fields constitute a substantial fraction of the total flagged pixels, the associated processing challenges are concentrated predominantly within $\sim$\,15\degr\ of the Galactic plane.

As the survey continues, crowded fields will comprise an increasingly large fraction of the observed sky.
Robust processing of these fields is therefore essential to fully realizing the scientific goals of \gls{LSST}.
To address this challenge, a dedicated crowded-field processing \gls{pipeline} is currently under development.
Early results are promising: regions that appear as merged detections in the standard \gls{pipeline} can instead be successfully deblended into their constituent sources, enabling individual detection and measurement.
The pipeline is currently undergoing testing, with integration into the LSST \gls{Science Pipelines} for general use targeted within the next year.

\subsection{Stellar Photometry}
Comparison with an external photometric catalog is a key test of photometric quality, particularly of the absolute \gls{flux} scale.
Such a comparison is, however, far from straightforward, not least because any external catalog will also have its own \gls{calibration} systematics.
Further, outside the deep fields, any comparison would be restricted to relatively bright stars and would therefore not be representative of the full \gls{DP2} depth.
A proper treatment of these limitations is beyond the scope of this paper, and is left to a dedicated future paper on photometric performance.
Instead, we assess the internal consistency of the \gls{DP2} photometry using the repeatability of per-visit measurements and the stellar loci measured on the Deep Coadds.
Both are differential measures (i.e., between visits or between bands), so they test the relative photometric \gls{calibration} but are insensitive to any error in the absolute flux scale.

Since most of the DP2 \gls{footprint} was observed multiple times, the repeatability of photometric measurements of the same star across visits provides a test of the consistency, and hence reliability, of the per-visit photometric calibration.
We measure this repeatability using the \gls{PSF} photometry of bright (SNR~$>200$), isolated, unresolved objects, the vast majority of which are stars.
To keep the calculation computationally feasible, it is performed per \gls{tract}: for each star with at least three measurements from the visits covering a given \gls{tract}, we calculate the standard deviation of those measurements, and then take the median over all stars in the \gls{tract}.
This corresponds to the PA1 metric defined in the LSST Science Requirements Document \citep[][]{LPM-17}.
Following recalibration using the \gls{FGCM} fits, the median PA1 values across all tracts with coverage are 9.1, 7.1, 6.8, 5.9, 5.8, and 6.2~mmag in the $ugrizy$ bands, respectively, meeting the minimum specifications of the Science Requirements \gls{Document}.
%\todo{The FGCM zero point errors will be added to give context to how much they are contributing to these PA1 values.}

\figref{fig:dp2_stellar_loci} shows the $ugriz$ stellar loci of unresolved objects in the Deep Coadds, selected using the \gls{Object} table's \texttt{extendedness} parameter (\secref{sec:drp-multivisit-detection}).
The tightness of these loci indicates the good quality of the band-to-band relative photometry in the Deep Coadds.

\subsection{Coadd Depth, Completeness and Purity}

The depth of coadds can be estimated in several different ways, as well as separately for point and extended objects.
One method is to simply measure statistics like the median/mean magnitude of objects in a narrow range of significance, i.e. at $5\sigma \pm 0.1\sigma$.
A drawback of this method is that uncertainties in coadd fluxes are typically underestimated, and by a larger factor for extended objects.

Injecting synthetic objects into real data is another method that was used for \gls{DP1}.
However, the switch to cell-based coadds necessitated changes to the injection methodology for \gls{DP2} and a large-scale injection campaign has not yet been completed.
Preliminary analysis of injection into the central tract of the \gls{COSMOS} \gls{DDF} yields about 70\% completeness for point sources at $r=26.5$, the 5$\sigma$ point--source limit reported in \tabref{tab:dp2_ddf_m5_depths}.

\begin{figure}[tpb]
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/dp2_ecdfs_euclid_completeness}
\caption{Completeness and incorrect classification fraction as a function of Euclid $VIS$-band S\'ersic magnitude.
The ``Incorrect Class'' line shows objects that are matched but classified incorrectly by \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness}.}
\label{fig:dp2_ecdfs_euclid_completeness}
\end{figure}

The third method for measuring completeness is to match objects against comparable or deeper reference catalogs, where higher-resolution data from space-based telescopes is preferred.
For this method, as for \gls{DP1}, we use a greedy, probabilistic matching algorithm that matches reference objects in order of descending brightness to the most probable target within a $0\farcs5$ radius.

\figref{fig:dp2_ecdfs_euclid_completeness} shows completeness in the ECDFS field compared to Euclid Q1 \citep[][]{2026A&A...711A...1E,2026A&A...711A...4E}, along with the proportion of objects whose star/galaxy classification disagrees with Euclid.
Euclid stars are defined solely with a \texttt{mumax\_minus\_mag < -2.6} cut as recommended by \citet[][]{2026A&A...711A..20E}, whereas DP2 stars are selected with \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness} $< 0.2$.
Completeness peaks above 96.5\% for $20 < \mathrm{mag_{VIS}} < 21$, with a misclassification rate just under 1\%.
Purity, on the other hand, peaks at bright magnitudes but dips below 93.3\% at the same magnitude range.

Completeness and purity figures depend on the choice of flags to exclude possible spurious detections.
For Euclid, we select only objects with \texttt{spurious\_prob}$ < 0.2$.
This cut excludes more objects than the default value of 0.5 recommended by \citet[][]{2026A&A...711A...4E}.
Visual inspection of the coordinates of Euclid detections with $0.2 <$ \texttt{spurious\_prob} $< 0.5$ revealed that most are clustered around bright stars and their diffraction spikes.

Similar problematic detections are found in the \gls{DP2} catalogs, many but not all of which are excluded using \texttt{coord\_flag}.
Not all of these flagged objects are necessarily spurious detections, but as with Euclid, many cluster around bright stars and have overestimated fluxes due to their footprints including part of the extended wings of the star's \gls{PSF}.
These complications generally make estimates of completeness and purity from matching to real catalogs more pessimistic compared to equivalent quantities from source injection.

\subsection{Galaxy Photometry and Sizes}

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/dp2_ecdfs_sersic_sizes}

\label{fig:dp2_ecdfs_sersic_sizes}

One of the main improvements in \gls{DP2} galaxy measurements is the use of an exponential fit to initialize the multiband S\'ersic fits.
\figref{fig:dp2_ecdfs_sersic_sizes} shows the distribution of S\'ersic size as a function of S\'ersic $i$-band magnitude in \gls{ECDFS}.
While there are still some overly large galaxy outliers, the proportion is fairly small.
The 99.5th percentile size increases slightly from 18th to 22nd magnitude, where the outliers are most problematic, but falls for fainter objects.
This is likely partly due to improved deblending as well; the Gaussian model in \gls{ShearObject}, which does limited masking of neighbors rather than multi-object modeling, has a considerably larger fraction of ``superspreaders''.

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/dp2_ecdfs_sersic_size_ratio_euclid}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/dp2_cosmos_sersic_size_ratio_cosmos2025}
\caption{S\'ersic size ratios for galaxies in ECDFS and COSMOS, as measured and classified in Euclid \gls{Q1} and COSMOS2025, respectively.}

Using \texttt{griz\_model\_extendedness}, the S\'ersic sizes of stars are also consistently smaller than galaxies until 25th magnitude.
At 24th magnitude, however, the number counts of stars drop precipitously, as the classifier's completeness drops to maintain purity.
The cause of the significantly nonzero sizes of stars is not entirely understood, but the median sizes are consistent with other forward modeling algorithms, including from CModel's exponential fits (which use an independent double shapelet \gls{PSF} model) and from the Gaussian model in \gls{ShearObject}.

\figref{fig:dp2_sersic_size_ratios} shows the ratio of S\'ersic sizes measured in DP2 with two space-based catalogs - Euclid Q1 in ECDFS and COSMOS2025 \citep[][]{2025A&A...704A.339S} in COSMOS.
In both cases, the median size ratio remains fairly consistent to about 24th magnitude, and the scatter is remarkably small for Euclid \gls{Q1} especially.
There is a shallow slope in the median such that bright galaxies tend to have smaller size ratios.
This is likely due to a combination of reasons, most notably the known \gls{background} oversubtraction which is less likely to be an issue in space-based data.
Faint galaxies have substantially overestimated sizes; by $i=25$ median effective radii (from COSMOS2025) drop to about $0\farcs22$, which is barely larger than the \gls{LSSTCam} pixel size.

In contrast with Euclid \gls{Q1}, where the median size ratio crosses unity around 23rd magnitude, DP2 S\'ersic sizes are systematically larger than those measured from COSMOS2025.
This is potentially due to filter differences, since Euclid sizes are derived from VIS data, which overlaps most of the $griz$ filter wavelengths other than the bluest part of $g$.
COSMOS2025 sizes, on the other hand, are measured on the \gls{JWST} NIRCAM filters (F115W, F150W, F277W, F444W), only the bluest of which even overlaps partially with the $y$ band.
Note that \gls{DP2} effective radii have been multiplied by a factor of $2\ln(2)$ or about 0.142 dex, due to a documented bug in the recording of the Gaussian mixture weights for the multiband S\'ersic and exponential models.
There is no evidence yet of any similar multiplicative offset in the COSMOS2025 or Euclid \gls{Q1} sizes, nor would it likely be the same factor since neither survey uses Gaussian mixture approximations to S\'ersic profiles.

\subsection{Shear Object catalog}
As described in \secref{sssec:objectshear} and \secref{sec:drp-multivisit-shearobject}, the \gls{ShearObject} catalog is produced by running the metadetection \gls{algorithm} on the coadded images.
It contains the lensing shear measured as ellipticity on the no-shear counterfactual, plus four subcatalogs from the counterfactuals in which a linear shear has been artificially applied; the latter are used to compute the shear response and selection biases.
A full validation of the Shear \gls{Object} catalog is beyond the scope of this paper and will be the focus of follow-up studies.
Nevertheless, a basic check is possible by exploiting the expectation that over large areas such as those covered by \gls{DP2}, the mean shear of the no-shear counterfactual should be zero.
We therefore measure the mean \texttt{gauss\_g1} of detections in the no-shear counterfactual, after excluding any with a flag set or with \texttt{is\_primary=False}.
In doing so, we obtain a mean \texttt{gauss\_g1} value that, while small, is significantly nonzero.
This offset persists when we restrict to the best detections (\texttt{mfrac<0.1} and \texttt{gauss\_snr>10}) and when we apply inverse-variance weights to downweight fainter detections.
Following further investigation, we attribute this offset to objects in the tails of the \texttt{gauss\_g1} distribution, which, from visual inspection, are typically bright stars and nearby galaxies.
Additional masking of nearby objects may need to be performed prior to any shear analysis.

Further validation of the Shear Object catalog can be performed by matching the no-shear catalog to the \gls{Object} catalog.
We stress that this is suitable for validation only; matching reintroduces the selection biases that metadetection is designed to remove, so the Shear \gls{Object} subcatalogs must not be matched to one another or to the \gls{Object} catalog in any shear analysis.
For this check, we compare the \texttt{pgauss} $g-i$ color of the no-shear catalog to the \gls{GAaP} $g-i$ colors in the \gls{Object} catalog.
As these are designed to measure comparable quantities, we expect the two to largely agree, so any major disagreement would point to a problem with one of the two measurements.
This comparison, shown in \figref{fig:dp2_shear_pgauss_gaap}, reveals good agreement for $r \lesssim 23$, with a median offset (\gls{GAaP} $-$ \texttt{pgauss}) of $\sim -20$~mmag; at fainter magnitudes, both the offset and the scatter increase.

\subsection{Difference Imaging}
\gls{DP2} includes over 12,000 visits with \gls{DIA} Sources.
Per \secref{sec:drp-variabletransient-artifact}, reliability scores are calculated for each \gls{DIA} \gls{Source}, but no cuts are applied.
As a result, the majority of \gls{DIA} Sources have low reliability scores ($< 0.5$) and are not astrophysical in origin.
Common causes of low-reliability \gls{DIA} Sources include low-quality \gls{Template} Coadds (e.g., single visit self-subtractions) and poorly constrained background sky levels due to scattered light or crowded fields.

In some circumstances, spurious \gls{DIA} Sources can appear in a grid pattern reminiscent of the gaps between detectors on the focal plane.
This can occur when \gls{Template} Coadds have a sudden change in PSF size.
This discontinuity cannot be well represented with a smooth matching kernel during difference imaging source measurement, and a too-large \gls{PSF} can produce spurious positive flux \gls{DIA} Sources in the narrow grid strips where the \gls{PSF} is small.
There is presently no flag or column to identify these, but we expect it to be less prevalent for \gls{Template} Coadds that are deeper (i.e., those with larger numbers of overlapping input visits).

Most of the visits with \gls{DIA} Sources in \gls{DP2} have fewer than 2000 \gls{DIA} Sources with reliability $>0.5$.
The outliers with excess ``reliable'' \gls{DIA} Sources include: (1) a handful of visits listed in the Release Documentation Known Issues when the telescope was moving while the shutter was open, (2) visits affected by the template \gls{PSF} discontinuity problem described above, and (3) visits in crowded and/or nebulous fields where it is challenging to robustly measure the background flux level.

\subsubsection{DIA Source detection completeness}
Following and extending the approach in DP1~\citep[][]{rtn-095}, we assess the performance of difference imaging by injecting synthetic sources into a subset of DP2 science images, carrying out difference imaging, and measuring properties of the recovered detected synthetic sources.
We consider a subset of visits across the entire \gls{DP2} sky in all six filter bands for this investigation.
We first create a catalog of synthetic sources, which we then inject into the science images and process through the difference imaging \gls{pipeline}.
%
In this sample we include sources that are both ``hosted'' and ``hostless'' synthetic sources, i.e., point sources embedded in a host galaxy and isolated point sources, each with randomly sampled location and brightness.
%
The hosted sources additionally have randomly selected positions within their host galaxy and position angles, as well as brightnesses that are randomly selected relative to the host galaxy brightness.
%
A fraction of these sources are ``template'' injections, meaning they are injected into the template coadd image and thus when obtaining the difference image they appear with negative \gls{flux}.
%
There is also a fraction of ``variable source'' injections, meaning they are injected into both the template and visit images, but with different random brightnesses, so that they can appear with both negative and positive fluxes in the difference image.
%
Overall, we inject approximately 300,000 synthetic sources into 1990 \gls{DP2} visits, and calculate recovery statistics.
%
After injection we run a \gls{forced photometry} stage on the locations of our synthetic sources, in order to obtain an empirical estimate of the \gls{SNR} of each injected source, and also to clean the sample of synthetic sources of those that had flags like \texttt{BAD}, \texttt{SATURATED}, or \texttt{SPIKE} set in the pixels of the injected source.
This is the ``nominal'' sample of injected synthetic sources.
%
We perform a double mutual match of the injected synthetic sources to the detected \gls{DIA} Sources, and determine which injected sources were successfully recovered, using a $0\farcs5$ matching radius.

Our initial completeness analysis showed that many high-\gls{SNR} synthetic sources were not recovered.
We examined numerous individual unrecovered \gls{DIA} Sources to determine common underlying causes, and found two prominent ones.
First, in situations where a synthetic source was injected into a template image relatively close to where an unrelated synthetic source was injected into a visit image, the resulting difference image creates a blended \gls{footprint} that can appear as one extended source or like a lopsided dipole.
One of the two injected synthetic sources is not recovered in this scenario, likely because deblending is not yet implemented in difference imaging (see \secref{sssec:DIASource}).
Second, in situations where a synthetic source was injected close to an image region with certain pixel flags (e.g., \texttt{SATURATED}, \texttt{SPIKE}), they are not recovered.
This is intentional because many sources close to such masked regions are bogus detections originating from subtraction artifacts.
To identify affected sources, we define a seven-arcsecond wide region around all injected synthetic \gls{DIA} Sources, and note whether any of the pixels in the region had any pixel flags set.
If any of the pixels in this buffer region have one or more of \texttt{INTERPOLATED}, \texttt{SATURATED}, \texttt{BAD}, \texttt{SATURATED\_TEMPLATE}, or \texttt{SPIKE} set, we exclude the injected synthetic source from the recovery analysis.

To characterize the completeness, we fit a logistic function to the fraction of recovered synthetic sources as a function of injected \gls{SNR}.
%
We do this separately for each filter and for each type of injection (visit, template, and variable), both for the nominal sample and for a mask-filtered sample from which sources affected by blending overlaps or pixel flag adjacency have been removed.
%
For the nominal sample, the fitted asymptotic completeness ranges from 93\% to 97\% across filters and injection types, with variable-source injections the least complete in every filter.
%
Removing the two known causes of failed recovery raises the asymptotic completeness to 96--99\%.
%
In all cases, 50\% completeness is reached at an injected \gls{SNR} of 4.8--5.0, consistent with the $5\sigma$ detection threshold applied to the difference images (\secref{sec:drp-variabletransient-dia}).

We assess the photometric accuracy of the recovered synthetic sources by comparing the measured fluxes to the injected fluxes.
\figref{fig:dp2_dia_fakes_photometry} shows the magnitude differences recovered for all sources in the mask-filtered sample, as a function of injected magnitude true values.
%
We also report the pull distribution, which is the difference between the measured and injected fluxes divided by the measured \gls{flux} uncertainty, and provide its mean and standard deviation as a function of injected magnitude as well.
%

\subsection{Solar System}\label{sec:drp-variabletransient-solar-association}
There are 299,343 solar system Objects (i.e., objects measured at least once by Rubin) in \gls{DP2}, representing almost 20\% of the known solar system.
Although 136,608 objects are observed at least once in each $griz$ band, detailed analysis of asteroid colors would be premature with \gls{DP2}.
Observations with different filters are not simultaneous, meaning the objects' rotational \gls{flux} variations add scatter to the measurement of their colors.
Given sufficient data, this can be overcome by fitting a rotational light curve for each individual object.
However, most objects in \gls{DP2} have too few measurements for a light-curve fit.
Hence, individual colors and H magnitudes are uncertain at the level of rotational \gls{flux} variations, which are typically much larger than the nominal uncertainty \texttt{HErr}, since the latter accounts only for the errors on instantaneous \gls{flux} measurements.

The ensemble colors of the solar system Objects are broadly plausible for an asteroid sample: median $g-r \sim 0.58$, $r-i \sim 0.18$, and $i-z \sim -0.01$~mag.
Implausible outliers are rare ($\sim 0.25$\% of all color measurements) and likely result from sparse phase fits heavily affected by individual photometric outliers.
These photometric outliers may result from self-subtraction when the template images are shallow and contain \gls{flux} from the asteroid.
Like the problem of insufficient measurements for rotational light-curve solutions, this issue of shallow templates causing self-subtraction and occasional photometric outliers should naturally disappear over future Data Releases based on larger numbers of input images.

There are 8.1 million \gls{DIA} Sources associated with solar system objects in DP2, representing about 2\% of all MPC measurements.
There is a persistent $\sim30$~mas bias between DP2 astrometric measurements and \gls{ephemeris} positions of MPC orbits.
When Rubin astrometry was compared instead to orbits fit only to \gls{Gaia} astrometry, the $\sim30$~mas bias disappeared.
After substantial investigation and discussion with both the MPC and JPL, we concluded that the bias is \textbf{not} in Rubin \gls{astrometry} (to $\le$~1~mas), but in the orbit catalog.
While $30$~mas is small, the median astrometric uncertainty on a DP2 DIA \gls{Source} associated with a solar system \gls{Source} is just 56~mas, making $30$~mas a substantial offset in the context of Rubin precision, thus highlighting the extent to which Rubin astrometry can improve asteroid orbits over time.
